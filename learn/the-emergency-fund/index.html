The emergency fund

How this was researched and written

Three to six months is the standard answer. The part that gets it wrong is what you multiply by.

An emergency fund (cash set aside for when things go wrong) is money you hold in cash, separate from everything else, for the specific purpose of absorbing a shock without selling your investments or borrowing.

It has one job, and it is not the job most people think. It is not a savings goal and it is not a low-return investment you are tolerating. It is the thing that stops a temporary problem from becoming a permanent financial setback.

What it actually protects you from

Without one, a job loss or a $4,000 car repair has to be funded from somewhere, and there are only two somewheres: selling investments, or borrowing.

Selling investments to cover an emergency is worse than it sounds, because emergencies correlate with bad markets. Redundancies cluster in downturns, and downturns are exactly when your holdings are at their lowest. You are forced to sell at the worst price, you crystallise the loss permanently, and the recovery happens without you in it.

Borrowing is worse still. It converts a one-off shock into an ongoing repayment that reduces your disposable income (what's actually left over to save or invest) for years, and for a Muslim investor it usually means an interest-bearing product, which raises a problem the emergency itself did not have.

Three to six months of what, exactly

The standard guidance is three to six months of expenses. The guidance is correct. What people get wrong is the thing being multiplied.

The common error is to build the fund on a stripped-back survival budget: rent, minimum groceries, utilities, nothing else. It produces a satisfyingly small target and it is the wrong target.

Base it on your real lifestyle expenses, including discretionary spending. The purpose of the fund is to carry your actual life through a shock, not to fund a bare-minimum existence while you are already dealing with a redundancy or an illness. Someone who has just lost their job does not also need to cancel every social commitment, stop seeing family, and eat rice for four months. That is the moment stability matters most, not least.

There is a practical argument here too, not just a humane one. A fund that requires you to live stripped-back to make it work will be abandoned early, because living that way is unsustainable, and the abandonment happens at exactly the wrong moment. A fund built on your real number gets you through.

So the multiplier is your real monthly spending from the previous lesson, the one that included the irregular items.

Where you sit on the three to six range

SituationLean toward
Single, no dependants, stable in-demand skills, could find work in weeks3 months
Stable salaried job, no dependants, some flexibility in your spending3 to 4 months
Dependants, or one income supporting a household6 months
Variable or commission income, self-employed, or a narrow specialisation where roles are scarce6 months or more

The logic is just two questions: how long would it realistically take to replace the income, and how many people are relying on it while you do.

Amina's fund, before and after

Amina spends $51,880 a year, which is $4,323 a month. At 30, single and salaried, three months is the right setting for her: about $13,000.

Later in this course she gets married and has a child. Nothing about her discipline changes, but the calculation does, because a second person now depends on that income and the consequences of a gap are no longer hers alone to absorb. She moves to six months, about $26,000.

Note what that shift is not. It is not caution replacing ambition. Her investing continues throughout. She topped the fund up over several months from the same disposable income (what's actually left over to save or invest), treating it as a one-off rebuild rather than a permanent redirection of her investing.

Where to keep it

Two requirements, and only two. It needs liquidity (how quickly you can turn it into cash), meaning you can access it within a day or two, and it must not be able to fall in value, which rules out having it invested.

For a Muslim investor there is a third consideration: the default Australian answer is a high-interest savings account, and interest is a problem in its own right. For a Muslim investor, look for a savings or transaction account that does not pay interest at all. That is the clean answer and it is available: several Australian banks offer non-interest-bearing everyday accounts, and the fund is not there to earn a return anyway. Its job is to sit still and be available.

Two boundaries worth setting now

  • It is not a holiday fund or a car fund. A known future expense is a savings goal, and it should be a separate pot with its own name. If the emergency fund (cash set aside for when things go wrong) gets used for planned spending, it will be empty when something real happens.
  • If you are carrying consumer debt, that comes first. Hold a small starter buffer of around $1,000, clear the consumer debt, and only then build the full three to six months. You cannot build wealth from a negative position, and holding cash that earns nothing while paying a lender for the privilege is a losing trade in both directions. The full ordering is in the next lesson, and the practical method is in the debt payoff guide.

Check yourself

3 questions on this lesson. Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere.

  1. 1Why should an emergency fund be based on real lifestyle expenses rather than a bare-survival budget?

    The point is maintaining stability through a disruption, not surviving it. A fund that only works if you live on rice will fail in practice.

  2. 2Amina spends $4,323 a month. She is single and salaried at 30, then later marries and has a child. What happens to her target?

    The trigger is dependants, not spending. Another person now relies on that income, so the window she needs to cover doubles.

  3. 3Why is selling investments to cover an emergency particularly bad?

    Redundancies cluster in recessions. The forced sale crystallises the loss permanently, which is the exact outcome the fund exists to prevent.

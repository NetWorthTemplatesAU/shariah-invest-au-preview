Every purchase is traded time

How this was researched and written

Price tags are in dollars. You do not earn dollars, you earn dollars per hour of your life, and that is the honest unit.

There is one conversion that changes how people spend, and it is not a budgeting rule. It is a unit change.

You do not earn money. You exchange hours of a finite life for money, at a fixed rate, in a job you may or may not enjoy. So the honest price of anything is not its dollar figure. It is the number of hours you must trade to obtain it.

Working out your real hourly rate

The number on your payslip is not it. You need the after-tax figure, because the tax portion was never available to spend.

Take Zaid. $80,000 gross. On the 2026-27 resident rates, that is $14,520 in income tax and $1,600 in Medicare levy, leaving $63,880 take-home. A standard Australian full-time week is 38 hours, so 1,976 hours a year.

$63,880 divided by 1,976 hours is $32.33 an hour, after tax.

That is the real exchange rate, and it is already lower than most people assume. It is also generous, because it ignores commuting, unpaid overtime, and the cost of being employed at all.

The SUV, priced in hours

Zaid's $65,000 SUV, at $32.33 an hour, against the used car Amina bought instead:

MeasureZaid's $65,000 SUVAmina's $18,000 used car
Hours of workabout 2,011about 557
38-hour weeksabout 53about 15
Working yearsjust over 1about 4 months

Read the last row again. Zaid traded more than a full year of his entire working life, every hour of it, with nothing left over for rent or food, for a vehicle.

That is not an argument that he should not own a car. It is an argument that $65,000 and $25,000 are not "a few hundred a month apart", they are about twelve months of his life apart. The dollar framing hides that. The hours framing cannot.

The reframe that lands hardest. Stop thinking of it as buying a more expensive car. Zaid bought the $25,000 car and also took a full year off work, unpaid. That is what the extra $40,000 purchased, priced in the only currency that is genuinely finite. Asked that way, the question answers itself for most people: would you rather have the nicer car, or the year?

The excitement wears off faster than you think

There is a well-documented pattern here worth naming: hedonic adaptation (the excitement wearing off faster than you think).

The satisfaction curve of a major purchase peaks before you own it. The anticipation, the research, the comparing, the deciding, that is the enjoyable part. Ownership starts high and then normalises fast. Within a few months the extraordinary thing is the ordinary thing, and it is now just the car you drive.

Two consequences follow, and they are practical rather than moralising:

  • The upgrade is worth less than it feels like at the moment of deciding. You are comparing today's excitement against a baseline you will return to in three months regardless of which option you choose.
  • The repayment does not adapt. The excitement normalises in months. The roughly $620 a month lasts seven years. That asymmetry is the whole trap.

Hobbies are not the problem

This is a necessary distinction, because arguments like this one often collapse into telling people to enjoy nothing.

There is a real difference between spending on a hobby you actively engage with and accumulating possessions for their own sake. Someone who buys a good bike and rides it every weekend has converted hours of work into hours of use, skill and health. Someone who buys a $4,000 camera that lives in a cupboard has converted hours of work into an object.

The test is not price, and it is not about denying yourself things. It is whether the thing is used, and whether you would still want it if nobody could see that you owned it. Both hobbies and status purchases cost the same hours. Only one gives the hours back.

Depreciation, and the other thing that $65,000 could have been

Cars lose value predictably. A new car in Australia typically drops 15% to 20% in the first year, and depreciation (the thing losing value as it ages) is steepest across the first three years before flattening out, per Savings.com.au and Budget Direct.

Modelling Zaid's $65,000 SUV on that pattern, and ending around 15% to 20% of the original price at ten years:

The right-hand column is not a prediction, it is an opportunity cost (what you gave up by choosing this instead) calculation: what the same money would have become at the course's standard 10% assumption. The two columns are about $157,000 apart at year ten, on a single decision made once, at 30.

Note also what the right-hand column would eventually produce: capital gains (the profit when you sell for more than you paid), which is the profit if it were sold above what was paid. The left-hand column produces the opposite by design.

The honest version of this comparison. Zaid genuinely needed transport, so the fair comparison is not $65,000 against nothing. It is $65,000 against Amina's $18,000 used car, with the $47,000 difference invested. That difference alone becomes roughly $122,000 over ten years at 10%. The car is not the mistake. The gap between the car he needed and the car he bought is the mistake, and it is worth about $122,000 of his future.

How to actually use this

Not for groceries. Pricing a $6 coffee in hours is a route to misery and it is not where the money is anyway.

Use it on anything above roughly a week of your take-home pay, where the decision is infrequent, the amount is large, and there is genuinely a choice. Work out your net hourly rate once, using the Tax Calculator if you want your own figure rather than Zaid's, then divide. Nine hundred hours is a number your brain understands in a way that $29,000 is not.

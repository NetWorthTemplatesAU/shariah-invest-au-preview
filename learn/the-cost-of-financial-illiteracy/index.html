The cost of financial illiteracy

How this was researched and written

Nobody sends you an invoice for what you did not know. You pay it anyway, in instalments, for decades.

Financial illiteracy does not feel like anything. There is no alert, no red letter, no moment where a system tells you that a decision you made at 25 will cost you six figures by 55. The bill arrives silently and in instalments.

This lesson is about making that bill visible, because the rest of the course is easier to sit through once you can see what is actually at stake.

Cost one: the years you did not use

The single most expensive thing in personal finance is starting late, and it is expensive because of compounding.

Compounding means your returns start generating their own returns. Year one, you earn a return on what you put in. Year two, you earn a return on what you put in plus last year's return. The effect is unremarkable for about a decade and then it stops being unremarkable.

Two people each invest $12,000 a year at a 10% long-run return. One starts at 25, stops contributing at 35, and never adds another dollar: $120,000 in total. The other starts at 35 and contributes every year until 65: $360,000 in total. At 65, the first person has roughly $3.34 million. The second has roughly $1.97 million. The person who contributed one third as much ends up with about 70% more, and the only variable was ten years of timing.

You can run this yourself on the site's Growth Calculator rather than taking the numbers on trust. Change the start age and watch how much of the final balance was never actually contributed by anyone.

Cost two: paying full price for the same product

The finance industry does not generally cheat people. It charges them different prices for the same thing, and the price you get is set by what you know to ask.

Concretely: two super funds can hold near-identical assets and charge fees a full percentage point apart. One percent sounds trivial. Over a 40-year working life, a 1% annual difference in fees on a growing balance typically consumes somewhere around a quarter of the final amount, because the fee compounds (returns earning their own returns) against you in exactly the same way returns compound for you. Nobody hides this. It is in the product disclosure statement. It goes unread.

Cost three: the one nobody warns you about

For a Muslim investor there is a fourth cost, and it is the one this site exists for. Not knowing how a product works means you cannot know whether you are complying with your own values.

The default case is the clearest example. If you have never chosen a super fund, your employer's contributions have gone into a default option since your first job. That default is a diversified portfolio that almost certainly includes conventional banks, insurers and interest-bearing debt instruments. Nobody deceived you. You were never asked, because the system is built to work without your input, and working without your input is exactly the problem here.

The guide Is my super halal? walks through how to actually check what your fund holds. The lesson on how super actually works covers it properly.

Cost four: decisions you avoided because you felt unqualified

This is the compounding (returns earning their own returns) cost of the other four. People who do not understand the vocabulary do not engage, and not engaging is itself a decision. Money sits in a transaction account for five years because choosing seemed risky. It was not risky. It was a guaranteed slow loss to inflation (your money buying less over time), which ran at 3.8% in the year to June 2026 per the ABS. One year of that is easy to shrug at. Five years of it is not: compounding the ABS June-quarter annual rates from 2022 to 2026 (6.1%, 6.0%, 3.8%, 2.1%, 3.8%) gives roughly 24% cumulative inflation over five years. In plain terms, something that cost $100 in mid-2021 costs about $124 today, so cash left sitting still lost about a fifth of what it could buy while nothing appeared to happen to it.

And underneath all of it sits the number most people never calculate: net worth (what you own minus what you owe). Not your salary, not your bank balance. Everything you own, minus everything you owe. Most people can recite their salary to the dollar and have never once calculated the number that actually describes their financial position.

The good news, and it is genuine

Every cost listed above is caused by missing information, not by lacking money, intelligence or discipline. Information is the cheapest thing on that list to fix. The next lesson makes the case that it is also much easier than the industry's vocabulary suggests.

Check yourself

2 questions on this lesson. Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere.

  1. 1Someone invests $12,000 a year from 25 to 35 and then stops. Someone else invests $12,000 a year from 35 to 65. Both assume a 10% return. What happens?

    Roughly $3.34 million from $120,000 contributed, versus $1.97 million from $360,000 contributed. Time in the market is doing most of the work, not the contribution size.

  2. 2Why does the lesson treat a 1% difference in annual fees as a serious problem?

    The mechanism is identical to compounding, just pointed the other way. It is disclosed in the PDS, it simply goes unread.

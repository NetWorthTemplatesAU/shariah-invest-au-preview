Inflation: the quiet tax on cash

How this was researched and written

The one loss that is guaranteed rather than possible, and the only one nobody ever panics about.

The last lesson was about a loss you can see. This one is about a loss you cannot, which is why it does far more damage to far more people.

Inflation (your money buying less over time) is the reason $50,000 sitting safely in an account is not sitting safely at all. It is shrinking, every day, by an amount too small to notice and too consistent to escape.

Where it actually stands

Australian CPI inflation was 3.8% in the twelve months to June 2026, with the trimmed mean measure at 3.6%. The more recent monthly figure showed 3.5% in the twelve months to July 2026. Housing was the largest contributor at 6.8%, followed by food and recreation at 3.3% each. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics.

Those figures are above the Reserve Bank's 2% to 3% target band. Whatever they do next, the number to plan around is not zero and has not been zero for a long time.

Why you cannot feel it

A 3.5% annual rise is roughly 0.3% a month. On a $6 coffee that is two cents. Nobody has ever noticed two cents.

What people notice instead is that things feel more expensive than they used to, without being able to point at when it happened, and they usually attribute it to their own spending. The mechanism is invisible at the timescale we perceive and enormous at the timescale we live.

Put $50,000 in an account earning nothing and leave it. At 3.5% a year, here is what it still buys:

YearsBalanceWhat it buys, in today's money
0$50,000$50,000
5$50,000$42,099
10$50,000$35,446
20$50,000$25,128
30$50,000$17,814

The balance never moves. Your statement shows $50,000 the whole time and no line item ever records a loss. After thirty years roughly two thirds of it is gone, and at no point did anything appear to go wrong.

This is the cash-is-safe myth in one table. Cash is stable, which is not the same as safe. Stability means the number does not move. Safety would mean the purchasing power does not move, and for cash in an inflationary environment that is guaranteed to be false.

Weighing the two risks properly

People compare investing against a false alternative. The comparison is not risk versus no risk. It is a possible loss with a possible gain, against a certain loss with no gain.

CashDiversified shares
Worst caseSteady, guaranteed erosionLarge fall, possibly for years
Best caseSteady, guaranteed erosionSubstantial real growth
CertaintyYou will lose purchasing powerWide range, historically positive over long periods
FeelsSafeRisky

Cash has the same worst case and best case, and the case is a loss. The distribution has no upside in it at all. Shares have a genuinely wide range of outcomes, including bad ones, and that range has historically included enough upside to more than cover inflation over long periods.

Which of these is riskier depends entirely on your time horizon (how many years until you need the money). Over one year, cash is obviously safer, because a 3.5% certain erosion beats a possible 30% fall. Over thirty, the ranking reverses completely and it is not close. This is the same principle as the previous lesson's five-year rule, seen from the other direction.

Which does not make cash wrong

Everything above is an argument against holding cash you will not need for decades. It is not an argument against holding cash.

Your emergency fund (cash set aside for when things go wrong) should be in cash, and the inflation it loses is the fee you pay for it being available on the day something goes wrong. Amina's $26,000 buffer loses a few hundred dollars of purchasing power a year, and it is what stops her selling investments at the bottom of a crash, which the previous lesson showed costs far more. That is a good trade and it should not be second-guessed.

Same for a house deposit in two years, a planned expense with a date attached, or anything else you have a near-term claim on. Money with a deadline belongs in cash and the erosion is the price of the certainty.

The problem is not cash. It is cash without a job. A balance nobody assigned a purpose to, sitting in an everyday account for eleven years because no decision was ever made about it, losing a third of its value to a process nobody watched.

  • Give every dollar of cash a job and a date. Emergency fund, a known expense, a deposit. Anything without a job is a decision you have not made yet.
  • Use the best available rate for the cash that has a job. For a Muslim investor this is not about chasing interest, which is riba, but about where the buffer sits: many deliberately hold it in a non-interest-bearing account and accept the erosion as the cost of compliance. the riba lesson covers that reasoning.
  • Plan in real terms, not nominal. A 10% return with 3.5% inflation is 6.5% of actual progress. Every long projection should be read with that subtraction in mind.
  • Revisit the buffer size as prices rise. Six months of spending is a moving target. A figure set five years ago is no longer six months.

To see the gap between nominal and real growth over your own timeframe, run the same projection twice in the growth calculator, once at your assumed return and once at that return minus inflation. The difference between the two lines is what inflation takes.

The point

Doing nothing is not neutral. Leaving money in cash is an active decision with a known, calculable cost, and it is the only decision in personal finance where the loss is certain rather than probable.

The reason it feels safe is that the number on the statement does not move. That is exactly what makes it dangerous, because a loss with no line item is a loss nobody ever reacts to.

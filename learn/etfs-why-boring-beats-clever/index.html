ETFs: why boring beats clever

How this was researched and written

The evidence on professional stock pickers is not close, and it has been consistent for decades across every major market including Australia.

This lesson is the general mechanics of funds and index investing. It deliberately does not cover Shariah screening of funds, which is a separate problem with its own failure modes and gets its own lesson in the Shariah-compliant vehicles lesson. Read that one too if you are screening.

What an ETF actually is

An index fund / ETF (a basket of many companies in one purchase) holds shares in many companies at once. You buy one unit of the fund, and that unit represents a slice of everything the fund holds.

A market index (a defined list of companies used as a scoreboard) is just a published list with rules. The S&P/ASX 200 is the 200 largest eligible companies on the Australian exchange, weighted by size. An index fund's job is unglamorous: hold what the list says, in the proportions the list says, and do nothing else.

The contrast is a managed fund (a fund where someone actively picks stocks for you), where a professional decides what to buy and sell, trying to beat the index. That service costs more, because analysts and research cost money.

The evidence, which is not ambiguous

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes a scorecard called SPIVA, which compares active funds against the index they claim to beat, in each market, over multiple periods. It has run for over two decades and it has an Australian edition.

The headline result for Australia, from the SPIVA Australia Scorecard: over a 15-year horizon, roughly 85% to 87% of Australian Equity General funds failed to beat the S&P/ASX 200, depending on which scorecard edition you read. Betashares' summary of the 21-year SPIVA record notes that in every category with a 10-year history, 75% or more of funds underperformed their benchmark.

Two things about that result deserve emphasis, because both are commonly missed.

  • The failure rate rises with time. Over one year a decent share of funds beat the index. Over fifteen, very few do. Luck is doing the work in the short window.
  • The funds that win one period are usually not the funds that win the next. S&P publishes a separate Persistence Scorecard specifically on this, and top-quartile performance has repeatedly shown little persistence. Which means last year's winner list is close to useless as a shopping list.

Why professionals lose to a list

It is not incompetence. The arithmetic is against them and the reasons are structural.

Fees compound against you. A managed fund charging 1.2% a year against an index fund charging 0.20% starts every year 1 percentage point behind before a single decision is made. The fee is charged on your whole balance regardless of whether the year was good.

All investors collectively are the market. Professional investors now do most of the trading. In aggregate they must produce the market return before costs, which means after costs they must produce less than it. Some individual managers beat the average, necessarily at the expense of others. Identifying which ones in advance is the hard part.

Trading costs and tax leak out. Every trade has a cost, and in Australia every sale at a profit outside super is a Capital Gains Tax (CGT) (the tax on that profit) event. A high-turnover fund realises gains constantly. An index fund that mostly holds still does not.

The high advertised return. Some managers genuinely do beat the index over long periods, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. The problem is selection in advance. Past returns are the only public evidence available and they have repeatedly failed to predict future returns. This is also exactly the shape of Zaid's problem: a fund marketed on a high past return is marketing a past number, and savings rate vs return rate takes that apart properly.

The question nobody can answer: when do you sell a winner?

Suppose you pick individual stocks and one of them doubles. Now what?

Sell, and you crystallise the gain, trigger CGT (the tax on that profit), and give up every future dollar the business earns. If it doubles again you watched it happen from outside. Hold, and you carry a position that is now a large share of your portfolio, and every further gain increases your concentration risk in a single company.

There is no rule that resolves this. Any rule you can state, sell at 50%, sell half, sell at a target price, is arbitrary. You will feel wrong either way, because after the fact one of the two paths will always have been better.

The index approach dissolves the question rather than answering it. The list rebalances mechanically. A company that grows becomes a larger weight because it got larger. A company that shrinks out of the index is removed. You are never asked to make the call, which matters more than it sounds, because that call is where a lot of self-inflicted damage happens.

What boring actually buys you

  • Instant diversification (not putting all your eggs in one basket) from one purchase, which the next lesson but one covers properly.
  • Low cost, which compounds in your favour every year rather than against you.
  • Almost no decisions, which removes most of the opportunities to be your own worst enemy.
  • Very little time, which is the real currency every purchase is traded time was about.

The catch worth stating plainly: a broad index fund is not screened for Shariah compliance. A standard ASX 200 fund holds the major banks, and they are several of its largest holdings. That problem, and what to actually check on a fund that claims to be screened, is the next module's job.
